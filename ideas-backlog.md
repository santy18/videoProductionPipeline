# Understanding ADHD — A Complete Syllabus

Oct 4, 2026

## Course overview

ADHD is a common, lifelong neurodevelopmental condition — not laziness, bad parenting or a lack of willpower. This 13-unit course explains what it is, how it shows up across life, how it is treated, and how to support people who have it.

By the end you should be able to:

1. Explain ADHD accurately, including the brain science behind it.
2. Recognize how it looks in children, teens, adults, and in girls and women.
3. Describe the main treatments and what the evidence says about them.
4. Challenge common myths and stigma with facts.
5. Support a friend, partner, student, coworker or child with ADHD — or understand yourself better.

Suggested arc: Units 1–4 cover the science and diagnosis; Units 5–7 cover the experiences that are often overlooked; Units 8–10 cover treatment and daily life; Units 11–13 cover strengths, stigma and support.

This syllabus is educational, not medical advice. Only a qualified clinician can diagnose ADHD or recommend treatment.

## Unit 1 — What ADHD is

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder is a neurodevelopmental condition marked by persistent inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interferes with daily life. It affects roughly 5–7% of children and about 2.5–3% of adults worldwide (approximate estimates; many adults are undiagnosed).

The DSM-5 describes three presentations, and a person's presentation can shift over time:

| Presentation | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Predominantly inattentive | Losing focus, forgetfulness, disorganization, losing things, daydreaming, missing details. Often missed because it is quiet. |
| Predominantly hyperactive-impulsive | Restlessness, fidgeting, talking a lot, interrupting, acting before thinking, trouble waiting. |
| Combined | Significant symptoms of both. The most commonly diagnosed presentation. |

- **The name is misleading:** it is less a *deficit* of attention than difficulty *regulating* it — people with ADHD can lock onto things that interest them (see hyperfocus, Unit 11).
- **A brief history:** first described by Sir George Still in 1902 as a "defect of moral control"; later called minimal brain dysfunction and hyperkinetic reaction; renamed ADD in 1980 and ADHD in 1987. Adult ADHD was widely recognized only from the 1990s onward.
- **Not new, not trendy:** rising diagnosis rates mostly reflect better recognition, especially of adults, girls and the inattentive type.

**Discuss:** Why might the inattentive presentation go unnoticed for years?

## Unit 2 — The ADHD brain and its causes

ADHD is one of the most heritable conditions in psychiatry, with genes explaining roughly 70–80% of the differences between people (twin-study estimates). It is caused by brain development, not by parenting, sugar or screens.

- **Executive function (Barkley; Brown):** the brain's "management system" — working memory, inhibition, planning, starting tasks, shifting focus, emotional control and time awareness. ADHD is often described as an executive-function and self-regulation condition.
- **Dopamine and norepinephrine:** differences in how these chemical messengers work affect motivation and reward. Boring tasks feel genuinely harder to start; interesting or urgent tasks feel easier.
- **Brain networks:** differences in the prefrontal cortex and in how the default mode network (mind-wandering) and task-focused networks switch on and off.
- **Brain maturation (Shaw et al., 2007):** some regions mature on average a few years later, but brain differences are small at the group level and cannot diagnose an individual.
- **Time blindness:** difficulty sensing time passing — the root of chronic lateness and "now vs not now" thinking.
- **Other risk factors:** premature birth, low birth weight, and prenatal exposure to alcohol or tobacco raise the risk somewhat.
- **Not causes:** sugar, vaccines, video games, poor parenting or lack of discipline. Parenting can shape how symptoms are managed but does not cause ADHD.

**Discuss:** How does "interest-based" attention explain why someone can game for hours but not finish homework?

## Unit 3 — Symptoms across the lifespan

ADHD does not disappear with age for most people — it changes shape. Visible hyperactivity often fades into inner restlessness, while inattention and executive-function struggles tend to persist.

| Life stage | Common signs |
| --- | --- |
| Early childhood | Constant motion, climbing, trouble sitting through stories, frequent accidents, intense tantrums |
| School age | Careless mistakes, unfinished work, losing belongings, blurting answers, trouble with friendships and turn-taking, "not living up to potential" comments |
| Teens | Procrastination, missed deadlines, risk-taking, impulsive decisions, driving accidents, mood swings, struggles as school demands rise |
| Adults | Chronic lateness, overwhelm, unfinished projects, job changes, money trouble, forgetting appointments, inner restlessness, difficulty relaxing, relationship strain |
| Older adults | Often mistaken for memory decline; may have gone a lifetime undiagnosed |

- **Persistence:** a majority of children with ADHD continue to have impairing symptoms in adulthood, even if they no longer meet every criterion.
- **Variability:** symptoms are worse in boring, unstructured or stressful settings and better with novelty, interest, urgency or one-to-one attention — which is why ADHD is often misunderstood as "selective" effort.
- **The cost of untreated ADHD:** higher rates of accidents, school dropout, unemployment, substance use and lower self-esteem — risks that treatment substantially reduces.

**Discuss:** Why might a child described as "smart but lazy" actually be struggling with ADHD?

## Unit 4 — Diagnosis

There is no blood test or brain scan for ADHD. Diagnosis is a clinical judgment based on history, symptoms and impairment, made by a psychiatrist, psychologist, pediatrician or other trained clinician.

DSM-5 criteria in brief:

- At least 6 symptoms of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity (5 for people aged 17 and older), lasting 6+ months.
- Several symptoms present before age 12.
- Symptoms appear in two or more settings (home, school, work, social life).
- Clear impairment in functioning.
- Not better explained by another condition.

A typical assessment:

1. **Clinical interview** about current struggles, childhood history and family history.
2. **Rating scales** completed by the person and others (for example the Vanderbilt for children, ASRS or CAARS for adults).
3. **Reports from others** — parents, teachers, partners — and old school reports when available.
4. **Ruling out look-alikes** such as anxiety, depression, sleep disorders, thyroid problems, trauma or hearing and vision issues.
5. **Diagnosis and plan** covering treatment options and support.

Why people get missed:

- High intelligence or strong support can compensate until demands outgrow coping strategies.
- Inattentive symptoms are quiet and less disruptive.
- Girls, women and adults were historically under-studied (Unit 5).
- Co-occurring anxiety or depression gets treated while the ADHD underneath goes unseen.
- Racial and cultural bias: behavior may be labeled defiance rather than ADHD in some groups.

**Discuss:** What are the risks of both over-diagnosis and under-diagnosis?

## Unit 5 — ADHD in girls and women

In childhood, boys are diagnosed roughly two to three times as often as girls, but in adulthood the gap narrows sharply — a sign that many girls were missed rather than unaffected.

- **Different presentation:** girls more often have inattentive symptoms — daydreaming, chattiness, disorganization — rather than disruptive hyperactivity.
- **Masking:** working extra hard, people-pleasing, perfectionism and anxious over-preparation hide struggles at a high personal cost.
- **Misdiagnosis:** often first diagnosed with anxiety, depression, eating disorders or borderline personality disorder.
- **Late diagnosis:** many women are diagnosed in their 30s or 40s, often after their child is diagnosed. Common reactions are relief, grief for lost years, and self-understanding.
- **Hormones:** estrogen affects dopamine, so symptoms can worsen before periods, after childbirth and around perimenopause.
- **Researchers to know:** Kathleen Nadeau, Ellen Littman, Stephen Hinshaw (long-term studies of girls with ADHD).

**Discuss:** How might gender expectations — "girls should be quiet and organized" — hide ADHD?

## Unit 6 — Emotions, rejection sensitivity and self-esteem

Emotional dysregulation is not in the official diagnostic criteria, yet many researchers (notably Barkley) consider it a core part of ADHD and one of its most impairing features.

- **Emotional dysregulation:** feelings arrive fast and strong, with less brake — quick frustration, irritability, overwhelm, and big excitement too.
- **Rejection sensitivity:** intense pain from real or perceived criticism or rejection. "Rejection sensitive dysphoria" (RSD) is a popular term coined by William Dodson; it is widely reported by people with ADHD but is not an official diagnosis and has limited formal research.
- **The criticism gap:** an often-repeated, unverified claim is that children with ADHD hear thousands more negative messages than peers by age 12. Whatever the true number, years of "try harder" and "why can't you just" commonly lead to shame.
- **Self-esteem:** chronic underachievement relative to ability can build a belief of being broken or lazy.
- **Burnout:** masking and constant compensating can lead to exhaustion.
- **Protective factors:** early diagnosis, understanding, strengths-focused feedback and supportive relationships.

**Discuss:** How does knowing ADHD affects emotions change how you might respond to someone's outburst?

## Unit 7 — Co-occurring conditions

Most people with ADHD have at least one other condition, which can complicate diagnosis and treatment. Rough ranges below vary widely across studies.

| Condition | Link with ADHD |
| --- | --- |
| Anxiety disorders | Very common; can be a reaction to ADHD struggles or a separate condition |
| Depression | Higher lifetime risk, often tied to chronic stress and low self-esteem |
| Autism (AuDHD) | Substantial overlap; both diagnoses allowed together only since DSM-5 (2013) |
| Learning disorders | Dyslexia, dyscalculia and dysgraphia often co-occur |
| Sleep problems | Delayed sleep phase and insomnia are very common |
| Substance use | Higher risk when ADHD is untreated; treatment is linked to lower risk |
| Oppositional defiant disorder | Common in children with hyperactive or combined ADHD |
| Tic disorders and Tourette's | Frequent overlap |
| Binge eating | Linked to impulsivity and reward-seeking |

Why it matters: treating only the anxiety or depression may leave ADHD untouched, and vice versa. A good assessment looks at the whole picture.

**Discuss:** How might untreated ADHD lead to anxiety or depression over time?

## Unit 8 — Treatment: what works

ADHD is highly treatable. The strongest evidence supports medication combined with education, skills and support — tailored to each person by a clinician.

| Treatment | What it is | Evidence and notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stimulant medication | Methylphenidate (e.g. Ritalin, Concerta) and amphetamines (e.g. Adderall, Vyvanse) | Among the most effective treatments in psychiatry; most people respond to one type. Common side effects include reduced appetite and sleep trouble. |
| Non-stimulant medication | Atomoxetine, viloxazine, guanfacine, clonidine | Useful when stimulants don't suit; take longer to work. |
| Behavioral parent training | Teaches parents consistent routines, praise and consequences | First-line for young children; strong evidence. |
| Classroom supports | Behavior plans, accommodations, teacher strategies | Helps learning and behavior at school. |
| CBT for adult ADHD | Skills for planning, procrastination and unhelpful thinking (Safren; Solanto) | Good evidence for adults, especially alongside medication. |
| ADHD coaching | Practical goal-setting and accountability | Promising but less researched. |
| Lifestyle | Exercise, sleep, nutrition | Supports well-being; exercise has short-term focus benefits. Not a replacement for treatment. |

- **Medication myths:** stimulants taken as prescribed do not cause addiction in people with ADHD, and treatment is linked to lower substance-use risk.
- **Long-term outcomes:** large Swedish registry studies link medication to fewer accidents, injuries, criminal convictions and, in some studies, lower mortality.
- **Weak or no evidence:** elimination diets for most people, most supplements, and commercial "brain training" programs. Neurofeedback evidence is mixed.

**Discuss:** Why do you think medication for ADHD carries more stigma than medication for asthma or diabetes?

## Unit 9 — Everyday strategies

The core idea: don't rely on willpower and memory — build the environment to do the remembering. Barkley calls this putting help at the "point of performance."

**Time**

- Make time visible: analog clocks, visual timers, alarms for transitions.
- Estimate how long tasks take, then double it.
- Work in short sprints with breaks (Pomodoro-style).

**Starting and focusing**

- Break tasks into a first step so small it feels silly.
- Body doubling: work alongside someone, in person or online.
- Add urgency, novelty or reward: deadlines with others, music, a treat after.
- Reduce distractions: phone in another room, website blockers, noise-cancelling headphones.

**Organization**

- "A home for everything" near where it's used; clear bins and open shelving.
- One capture system for tasks, such as a single app or notebook.
- Automate bills and use recurring reminders.

**Routines and health**

- Consistent sleep and wake times; ADHD and sleep problems feed each other.
- Regular exercise and regular meals.
- Plan for the "wall of awful" (Brendan Mahan): the emotional build-up that makes starting avoided tasks feel impossible.

**Discuss:** Pick one strategy and try it for a week. What helped, and what got in the way?

## Unit 10 — School, work and relationships

With the right accommodations, people with ADHD can thrive in school and at work. In the US, ADHD can qualify for legal protections; rules differ by country.

**School (US)**

- **Section 504 plans:** accommodations for students whose ADHD limits learning.
- **IEPs under IDEA:** specialized instruction, often under "Other Health Impairment."
- **Common accommodations:** extended test time, a quiet testing room, preferential seating, broken-up assignments, movement breaks, written instructions, check-ins.
- **College:** contact the disability services office; documentation is usually required.

**Work**

- The Americans with Disabilities Act can cover ADHD; reasonable accommodations are requested through HR.
- **Examples:** noise-cancelling headphones, a quieter workspace, written task lists, flexible hours, regular check-ins, remote work.
- **Disclosure is a personal choice** with pros and cons; some people ask for accommodations without naming ADHD.
- **Good-fit careers** tend to involve variety, urgency, hands-on work or passion — but people with ADHD succeed in every field.

**Relationships**

- Common friction: forgetfulness read as not caring, unequal household load, impulsive spending, interrupting.
- The "parent-child dynamic" (Melissa Orlov): one partner becomes the manager, causing resentment on both sides.
- What helps: naming ADHD as the shared problem rather than the person, shared systems and calendars, and couples work with an ADHD-informed therapist.

**Discuss:** What accommodation would be easiest for a teacher or manager to offer, and which would make the biggest difference?

## Unit 11 — Strengths and lived experience

ADHD brings real challenges, and many people with it also report real strengths. Both are true, and neither cancels the other out.

- **Commonly reported strengths:** hyperfocus on interesting tasks, creativity and divergent thinking, energy, spontaneity, humor, courage and risk-tolerance, calm in a crisis, curiosity, and resilience built from years of adapting.
- **The evidence:** strengths research is newer and mostly based on self-report (for example Sedgwick et al., 2019; studies on ADHD and entrepreneurship). Findings on creativity are promising but mixed.
- **Hyperfocus is double-edged:** it can produce deep work and also lost hours, missed meals and neglected priorities.
- **The neurodiversity movement:** views ADHD as natural human variation that becomes disabling partly because environments are built for other brains.
- **Balance:** a "superpower" framing can dismiss real suffering; a purely deficit framing ignores real abilities. Listen to how each person describes their own ADHD.
- **Lived experience:** read or listen to first-person accounts (see the reading list) to understand ADHD from the inside.

**Discuss:** What are the benefits and risks of calling ADHD a "superpower"?

## Unit 12 — Myths vs facts and fighting stigma

Stigma keeps people from seeking diagnosis and treatment. Correcting myths is one of the simplest forms of advocacy.

| Myth | Fact |
| --- | --- |
| ADHD isn't real; it's an excuse | Recognized by every major medical body, with decades of research on genetics, brain function and outcomes |
| Only hyperactive boys have it | Girls, women and adults have it too; the inattentive type is often invisible |
| Kids grow out of it | Most continue to have symptoms in adulthood, though they change |
| It's caused by bad parenting, sugar or screens | It is largely genetic and neurodevelopmental |
| They can focus when they want to | Attention follows interest and urgency, not choice |
| Medication turns kids into zombies | Correctly dosed medication should not; that is a sign the dose or drug needs adjusting |
| Medication leads to drug addiction | Treatment is linked to lower substance-use risk |
| Everyone is a little ADHD | Everyone gets distracted; ADHD means persistent, impairing symptoms across settings |
| People with ADHD aren't smart | ADHD occurs at every level of intelligence |
| It's overdiagnosed everywhere | Both over- and under-diagnosis occur; many adults and girls remain undiagnosed |

- **Language matters:** avoid using "so ADHD" as a joke or insult.
- **Social media:** has raised awareness but also spread oversimplified "signs you have ADHD" content; encourage professional assessment over self-diagnosis by video.
- **Awareness Month:** October is ADHD Awareness Month — a good time to share accurate information.

**Discuss:** Which myth have you heard most often, and how would you respond to it kindly?

## Unit 13 — How to support someone with ADHD

The most powerful support is understanding: seeing struggles as a brain difference rather than a character flaw.

**For everyone**

- Assume good intent; forgetting or lateness usually isn't a sign of not caring.
- Give information in writing and in small chunks.
- Offer body doubling or gentle reminders when asked — not nagging.
- Praise effort and strengths specifically, and often.
- Ask "What would help?" rather than assuming.

**For parents**

- Learn about ADHD and consider parent training programs.
- Use routines, visual schedules and immediate, consistent rewards.
- Separate the child from the behavior; protect their self-esteem.
- Partner with the school and advocate for supports.

**For teachers**

- Allow movement, chunk assignments, and check understanding privately.
- Use clear, predictable routines and frequent positive feedback.

**For partners, friends and coworkers**

- Build shared systems instead of keeping score.
- Don't take rejection sensitivity or emotional reactions personally; talk once things are calm.
- Look after your own well-being too; support groups exist for partners and families.

**What not to say:** "Everyone's a bit ADHD," "Just try harder," "You don't look like you have ADHD," or "Are you sure you need medication?"

**Discuss:** Think of someone you know who might have ADHD. What is one thing you could do differently?
